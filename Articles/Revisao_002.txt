# From AI-Assisted Coding to AI-Governed Software Engineering

Six months ago, I was using AI to help me write software.

Today, I believe I could rewrite a 700,000-line system in a month.

And that is not even the most important part.

The important part is understanding **why**.

Because those 700,000 lines were never the real asset.

Most of them are sediment: the same business concepts rewritten, protected, bypassed, and extended over decades.

The real asset was the collection of decisions buried inside the code.

And, until six months ago, most of those decisions lived in my head.

This is the story of how I started moving them out of my head and into an engineering system.

The month is just a consequence.

---

## The human brain is a great processor — and a terrible database

Software engineering requires a lot of processing.

We analyse problems, connect concepts, understand business rules, identify side effects, and search for solutions.

Humans are remarkably good at this.

But complex solutions are rarely linear.

A decision made in one place affects another process, which depends on another rule, which exists because of something that happened ten years ago.

That is where things become difficult.

The same brain that is good at processing information is not particularly good at remembering everything it has processed.

I increasingly believe that many engineering failures are not caused by a lack of intelligence.

They are caused by a **lack of memory**.

This is where the combination of humans and AI becomes interesting.

AI, without governance, can produce plausible, confident and subtly wrong solutions.

AI with governance can become something very different.

It does not forget the repository.

It does not get tired of reading the same documentation.

It does not decide that verification can wait until Monday.

So I stopped thinking about the division of work as:

> **AI writes. Human reviews.**

Instead, I think about it as:

> **I process. AI remembers. Governance is the contract between us.**

---

## Asking AI to fix the problem is often the first mistake

One of the biggest changes in my thinking was realising that the problem is not simply getting AI to generate better code.

The real problem is teaching AI to understand **what the software is supposed to do**.

That requires more than prompts.

It requires agents, skills, orchestration, validation and persistent knowledge.

Traditional documentation helps, of course.

But documentation has a fundamental weakness: someone still has to remember that it exists, find it, read it, understand it and keep it relevant.

Even organisations that produce enormous amounts of documentation still struggle with this.

So I started looking at agents differently.

An agent should not simply perform an action.

It should participate in a process where actions are recorded, validated and turned into reusable knowledge.

Two rules became particularly important for me:

**Consult before acting.
Retain after acting.**

If a task teaches us something, that knowledge should not disappear when the conversation ends.

It should return to the knowledge base through a defined chain:

**document → index → root rules → persistent memory**

Otherwise, as far as the engineering system is concerned, the learning never happened.

That was the point where I stopped thinking primarily about **prompt engineering** and started thinking about **memory engineering**.

The knowledge base became the asset.

The model became replaceable.

---

## Governance: memory without rules is just a pile of information

Having information is not enough.

A document does not automatically become an asset.

A memory does not automatically become knowledge.

There has to be governance.

One of the mechanisms I am experimenting with is simple:

Every problem discovered becomes a numbered question.

Every proposed correction is evaluated by a human.

It can be accepted.

It can be rejected.

But it should not simply disappear.

It becomes evidence that the subject was investigated.

The process continues until the open questions are resolved.

And there is another important principle:

**AI does not make the decision.**

If the AI encounters something it does not understand, it should not silently assume.

The gap becomes a question.

That distinction is extremely important when working with legacy software.

Because in a mature system, an assumption can be more dangerous than an obvious error.

The goal is to create a system where, six months from now, nobody needs to remember the details personally.

You should be able to ask:

> **“What do we know about subject X?”**

And have the engineering system reconstruct the knowledge that was accumulated around it.

---

## Lego pieces, not scale models

There is another lesson I am learning from working with AI on complex software.

AI is extremely good at generating structures.

Ask it for a model and, in seconds, it can produce an entire architecture that looks coherent, elegant and complete.

But there is a danger here.

AI does not inherently have a commitment to your business concept.

It recognises patterns and produces something similar to patterns it has seen before.

That can result in a beautiful model that is subtly wrong.

And with AI, the cost of creating that wrong structure has collapsed.

Previously, building the wrong structure could take weeks.

That cost forced us to think.

Today, AI can create the wrong structure in seconds.

So the risk of freezing the wrong model has actually **increased**.

My approach is therefore becoming less about asking AI to build complete structures and more about creating **Lego pieces**.

Small, well-defined concepts that can be combined in different ways without prematurely freezing the final architecture.

The best analogy I have found is a cloud.

A cloud is always recognisable as a cloud, regardless of its exact shape.

A well-defined domain concept should behave in a similar way.

Its identity should survive different combinations and implementations.

Before introducing a new domain object, I want AI to answer two questions — and I validate the answers:

**What is the single concept represented by this object?**

One sentence.

No “and”.

And:

**What fact does this object originate from?**

An object that exists primarily because someone created a database record is often a warning sign.

Another signal I use is even simpler:

> **If we need a rule whose only purpose is to prevent the structure from contradicting itself, the structure may already be wrong.**

This is not a documentation rule.

It is becoming part of the engineering process.

The idea is to turn this thinking into an agent that participates in the orchestration before a new domain object is introduced.

---

## What changed for me

Six months ago, I saw AI primarily as a tool that could make me faster at writing code.

Today, I see it as something much bigger.

The opportunity is not simply:

**“How much code can AI write?”**

The more interesting question is:

**“How much engineering knowledge can we preserve, organise, validate and reuse?”**

That changes the role of AI in legacy software dramatically.

A 700,000-line system is no longer just 700,000 lines of code.

It is a large collection of business decisions, historical constraints, relationships, exceptions and accumulated knowledge.

The challenge is not simply to rewrite it faster.

The challenge is to **extract the knowledge hidden inside it without losing the reasons behind the system**.

That is where I believe AI can become transformative.

Not as a replacement for the engineer.

Not as an autonomous programmer.

But as a persistent engineering partner that can remember what the team has learned, challenge gaps in understanding, enforce processes and make decades of accumulated knowledge accessible.

The code is only the visible layer.

**The real asset is the knowledge behind it.**

And I am increasingly interested in building the infrastructure that allows us to preserve it.
